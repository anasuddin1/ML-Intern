Abstract

Content Opportunity Scoring for Refresh Prioritization

This paper asks whether observable March 2026 search and engagement signals can help prioritize content observations for human review ahead of a measured April click decline. A Logistic Regression model was trained on ten March-only features and validated with a client-grouped train/test split to avoid client-level leakage. On the held-out test set of 5,062 observations, the model reached a ROC-AUC of 0.6132 and an average precision of 0.7813, against a majority-class baseline of 0.5000 and 0.6831 respectively on the same split. The strongest feature association was March CTR, followed by session volume and scroll activity, all interpreted as directional associations rather than causal effects. The resulting output is an evidence-aware ranked review queue — not an automatic content-change trigger — and it explicitly down-weights low-impression observations that would otherwise receive inflated probabilities. The work is offered as decision-support for a human reviewer, not as proof that any specific page requires a refresh.

Introduction

Problem Statement

Content teams manage far more pages than they can manually review every month. Search Console and analytics data already contain early signals — impressions, clicks, CTR, session engagement — that may be associated with a page's near-term trajectory, but without a systematic way to score and rank observations, review effort tends to default to recency or gut feel rather than evidence.

This project explores the Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring lane of the FlyRank Google Search Ranking & Discoverability capstone: using one month of observed search and engagement behavior (March 2026) to rank content observations for human review, evaluated against a retrospective next-month click-decline outcome (April 2026). The intent throughout is decision-support, not automated content management — every output in this paper is designed to be read, questioned, and acted on by a human reviewer, not executed automatically.

Section 01

Question & Decision

Research question. Can observable March 2026 search and engagement signals help prioritize content observations for human review when the next-month outcome is a measured decline in clicks?

Decision supported. The output is a ranked review queue. A content or SEO reviewer uses it to decide which observations deserve investigation first and which should remain in monitoring.

Unit of analysis. One client-content observation, aggregated across March 2026. The model target is the observed April click-decline outcome for observations that had March click demand.

Framing. This is prioritization, not automatic publishing or content modification. All recommended actions require human review.

Section 02

Data

The analysis uses the FlyRank internship warehouse release. Two monthly partitions are loaded — no other data is used.

Feature window: March 2026 (9,841,378 daily rows → 331,437 aggregated client-content observations).
Outcome window: April 2026 (10,424,730 daily rows → 362,172 aggregated observations).
Modeling dataset: 68,837 observations after inner join and target requirement.

March features aggregated per client-content pair: Google Search Console impressions, clicks, average position; GA4 sessions, engaged sessions, organic sessions, AI-referral sessions, scroll events; derived CTR and engagement rate.

Exclusions. Client and content identifiers are not predictive features. The client identifier is used only as a grouping boundary for honest validation. April outcome fields are not used as model features. No client names, domains, private queries, or credentials are published.

"Identifiers remain pseudonymous and are used only to establish the grouping boundary required for honest validation."

Section 03

Methodology

Target definition. An observation is labeled future_decline = 1 when its April clicks are lower than its March clicks, provided March clicks are greater than zero. Observations with no March click demand are excluded because the decline outcome requires measurable prior demand. Observed decline rate: 65.52%.

Model. Logistic Regression pipeline: median imputation → standard scaling → Logistic Regression (max_iter=1000, random_state=42). Logistic Regression was chosen for transparency: it produces probabilities, coefficients can be inspected and explained, and it does not reward complexity alone.

Validation design. GroupShuffleSplit with 20% test size and client-level grouping (random_state=42). Observations from the same client are kept entirely on one side of the split. This prevents the model from learning client-specific patterns in training and then appearing better on those same clients at test time.

Baseline. The baseline always predicts the training-set majority-class probability (0.6530) for every observation. This is deliberately naive and evaluated on the exact same grouped test split as the model.

Leakage controls. April outcome fields are not model features. future_decline is not a model feature. Client and content IDs are not predictive features. All features are March-only. Test clients have zero overlap with training clients.

Results vs Baseline

Both methods are evaluated on the same client-grouped test split of 5,062 observations. Because the test-set class balance is already high (68.3% positive), accuracy alone is not a strong discrimination measure. ROC-AUC and average precision provide more useful comparisons.

Section 05

Ranked Recommendations

A model probability by itself is not treated as an action priority. An important lesson from this project: observations with only one impression and one click can receive probabilities near 1.0 and appear at the top of a naive queue. That is not enough evidence to justify immediate content work.

This capstone adds an evidence-aware prioritization layer. The priority score = model probability × evidence factor × 100, where the evidence factor is log-scaled from March impressions (clipped 0–1). Low-evidence observations remain monitoring candidates regardless of model probability.

Section 06

Limitations & Honest Framing

The following claims cannot be made from this work:

  • !
    A content refresh caused a performance change. The April label is a retrospective measurement, not a refresh outcome.
  • !
    A refresh will reverse an observed decline. No intervention data is available.
  • !
    The model predicts Google's ranking algorithm. It measures observable signal associations, nothing more.
  • !
    A high model probability proves a page has a content problem. Probability reflects the model's learned pattern, not editorial quality.
  • !
    The model captures search intent, technical SEO, business value, editorial quality, or every reason a page changes over time.
  • !
    Sparse observations provide strong evidence for any action. Low-evidence rows are kept in monitoring.
  • !
    Results will generalize automatically to other time periods or client segments. This covers one March-to-April window with one validation setup. Additional time-based or repeated validation would be needed.

"On the evaluated March-to-April dataset and client-grouped test split, the Logistic Regression model produced measured classification performance for the defined future-decline outcome. These results provide directional evidence that March performance signals can support a content-opportunity scoring workflow. The model should be treated as decision-support rather than proof that a page should be refreshed or that a refresh will improve performance."
